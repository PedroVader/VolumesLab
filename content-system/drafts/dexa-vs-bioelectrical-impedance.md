# DEXA vs Bioelectrical Impedance: Which Measures Body Composition More Accurately?

DEXA and bioelectrical impedance analysis, usually shortened to BIA, estimate body composition using different physical measurements and different models. DEXA is commonly used as a reference method in comparison studies because it can estimate bone mineral, fat mass, and lean soft tissue. BIA is faster and more accessible, but its results depend heavily on the device, equations, and measurement conditions.

That does not mean every DEXA value is exact or every BIA value is unreliable. The more accurate method depends on what is being compared, which device is used, how the test is performed, and whether accuracy means agreement with a reference method or repeatability over time.

## How DEXA estimates body composition

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry is abbreviated to DXA and is also widely written as DEXA. The scanner passes two low-dose X-ray beams at different energy levels through the body. Differences in attenuation are used to estimate bone mineral and to separate soft tissue into fat and lean components.

Whole-body body-composition reports can include total and regional estimates. A bone-density examination may use the same underlying technology but a different scan protocol, region, and reporting purpose. A DEXA body-composition scan and a bone-density scan should therefore not be treated as identical appointments.

DXA has limitations. Results can be affected by the scanner model, calibration, software, positioning, movement, analysis settings, body size, and the way regional boundaries are defined. Values from different machines or software versions may not be directly interchangeable.

## How bioelectrical impedance estimates body composition

BIA sends a low-level electrical current through the body and measures impedance, which includes resistance and reactance. The device then combines the electrical measurement with a model and inputs such as height, weight, age, and sex to estimate total body water and body compartments.

There are many BIA designs. Some use foot-to-foot contact, some add hand electrodes, and some operate at multiple frequencies. The performance of one device should not be assumed to represent every BIA system.

Because body water is central to the calculation, changes in fluid distribution and measurement conditions can affect the estimate. Device equations and population assumptions can also create consistent differences from another method.

## DEXA and BIA compared

| Question | DEXA | Bioelectrical impedance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical measurement | Differential X-ray attenuation | Electrical impedance |
| Main estimated compartments | Bone mineral, fat, and lean soft tissue | Body water, fat-free mass, fat mass, and related outputs, depending on the device |
| Equipment | Dedicated scanner | Ranges from consumer scales to multi-electrode professional systems |
| Protocol sensitivity | Positioning, movement, calibration, software, and analysis settings | Device, electrode configuration, hydration and fluid distribution, posture, inputs, and equations |
| Cross-method comparison | Often used as a reference in studies | Agreement varies by device, compartment, and population |

## Accuracy, precision, and repeatability are different

Accuracy describes how close a result is to an accepted reference or target. Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree. Repeatability is a form of precision under the same conditions.

A BIA device can produce very similar values in repeated sessions while still showing a consistent offset from DXA. A published comparison of a standing BIA system, for example, reported high precision alongside systematic differences from DXA. That distinction matters because a stable trend is not the same as agreement between methods.

DXA is also an estimation method, not a direct chemical separation of every tissue in the body. Its usefulness as a reference does not remove the need for standardised operation and careful comparison.

## Why the numbers may not match

Two reports may use the same labels, such as body-fat percentage or lean mass, while defining and calculating the underlying compartments differently. The body position, measurement day, device, software, and model can also differ.

For this reason, a DEXA result and a BIA result should not be combined into one continuous trend as if they came from the same instrument. A method change can move the reported value even when there has been no equivalent change in the underlying body composition.

## Which method is more accurate?

For agreement with a research or clinical reference, DXA is generally the stronger comparator and is frequently used to evaluate BIA devices. The size and direction of the difference are not universal, however. They vary by BIA system, estimated compartment, study population, and protocol.

For repeated measurements, consistency of device and procedure is a separate consideration. A repeatable BIA system may be useful for observing a method-specific pattern, while still producing values that should not be substituted for DXA results.

The clearest interpretation therefore reports the method, device, and conditions alongside the number. It does not treat “body-fat percentage” as a method-independent fact.
