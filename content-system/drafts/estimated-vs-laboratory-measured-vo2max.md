# Estimated vs Laboratory-Measured VO2max: What Is the Difference?

An estimated VO2max and a laboratory-measured VO2max are produced in different ways. A wearable, fitness app, or field test uses performance data and an algorithm to estimate the value. A laboratory cardiopulmonary exercise test records respiratory gas exchange while exercise intensity increases under controlled conditions.

The two numbers may be close, but they are not interchangeable. To understand a result, it helps to know what information went into it, which exercise protocol was used, and whether oxygen uptake was measured directly from expired air.

## What an estimated VO2max is based on

An estimate does not normally measure oxygen uptake from the breath. It combines data that may include heart rate, pace, speed, movement, age, sex, height, weight, and a device-specific model.

The exact inputs depend on the product and activity. Apple, for example, states that compatible watches can estimate cardio fitness during an Outdoor Walk, Outdoor Run, or Hiking workout by using heart and motion sensors together with personal information entered in the Health app.

Field tests use a different estimation route. A result may be calculated from distance, time, speed, or heart-rate response during a standardised exercise task. In both cases, the output is derived from a model rather than from respiratory gas analysis.

This does not make an estimate meaningless. It means the result should be read as an estimate produced by a particular device, algorithm, and set of conditions.

## What a laboratory test records

During a laboratory cardiopulmonary exercise test, the person exercises on a treadmill or cycle ergometer while breathing through a measurement interface. The system records the volume and composition of inspired and expired air as the workload changes.

This allows oxygen uptake, carbon-dioxide output, and ventilation to be recorded throughout the test. Heart rate and other variables may also be collected, depending on the protocol and the purpose of the assessment.

The laboratory result is therefore based on observed respiratory gas exchange during that specific session. It is still affected by the equipment, calibration, protocol, exercise mode, test duration, and the person's response on the day.

## Estimated and laboratory-measured VO2max compared

| Question | Estimated VO2max | Laboratory-measured VO2max |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main input | Sensor, performance, and personal data | Respiratory gas exchange during exercise |
| Setting | Daily activity, a recorded workout, or a field test | Controlled treadmill or cycle test |
| Method | Device-specific or test-specific equation | Analysis of oxygen uptake and related gas-exchange variables |
| Output | An estimated value | A value measured during that laboratory protocol |
| Main limitation | Depends on model, inputs, signal quality, and supported activity | Depends on protocol, equipment, calibration, exercise mode, and test execution |

## Why the two results can differ

### They use different inputs

A wearable infers aerobic capacity from variables it can observe. A laboratory system records respiratory gases. Even when both outputs use the same units, the route to the number is different.

### The exercise mode may be different

A running estimate and a cycle-based laboratory test do not place the same demands on the body. The exercise mode and the person's familiarity with it can affect the value obtained during a specific test.

### The protocols are not identical

Workload steps, stage length, test duration, and the criteria used to identify the highest valid value can vary. Two laboratories can also use different analysers and processing rules.

### Wearable performance varies by device and person

Research comparing consumer devices with criterion measurements shows that average agreement across a group does not guarantee close agreement for each individual. Results are specific to the device, software version, activity, study population, and reference protocol.

### User data and sensor data matter

An incorrect weight, an incomplete heart-rate signal, or activity outside a device's intended estimation conditions can affect an algorithmic result. Manufacturers may also update their models over time.

## Can results be compared over time?

A sequence is easiest to interpret when it comes from the same method under similar conditions. A change from one watch model to another, from running to cycling, or from an estimate to a laboratory test introduces a method change as well as a possible physiological change.

For that reason, an estimated trend and a laboratory result should be labelled by method and date. They can sit in the same record, but they should not be treated as if they were repeated measurements from an identical protocol.
